Module 1: Copywriting foundations

The core ideas behind every good piece of copy, then three practice problems.

Minutes 0 to 6

The lesson

Copywriting is writing that moves one specific reader to take one specific action. Every piece needs one reader, one idea, one action.

Know the reader

Use your customer’s own words, taken from reviews, Reddit and emails. Then match the copy to their awareness level:

LevelYour copy should
UnawareReveal the problem
Problem-awareShow you understand it
Solution-awareShow why your approach is best
Product-awareRemove doubts, add proof
Most awareMake the offer and a clear CTA

Feature, benefit, outcome

  • Feature: 12-hour battery
  • Benefit: works all day without a charger
  • Outcome: no more hunting for a plug in meetings

Keep asking “so what?” until you reach something the reader cares about.

Headline formulas (10 to know)

Choose the formula that fits the reader’s awareness level, then test the result with the 4 U’s: is it useful, unique, ultra-specific and urgent (only when the urgency is real)? Clarity beats cleverness.

The frameworks (9 to know)

A framework is a proven order for your ideas, so you never face a blank page. They all copy the same reader journey: notice, care, believe, act.

FrameworkStepsBest for
AIDAAttention, Interest, Desire, ActionAds, emails, landing pages
PASProblem, Agitate, SolutionReaders who feel the problem; short ads
BABBefore, After, BridgeTransformations, emails
FABFeature, Advantage, BenefitProduct pages and bullets
4 PsPicture, Promise, Prove, PushHomepages, sales letters
PASTORProblem, Amplify, Story and solution, Transformation and testimony, Offer, ResponseLong sales pages
QUESTQualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, TransitionWebinars, articles, lead magnets
Star, Story, SolutionIntroduce a person, tell their struggle, show the fixAbout pages, case studies

Which one should I use?

  • Not sure: start with AIDA.
  • Reader feels the pain: PAS. Reader wants a change: BAB.
  • Writing bullets or a product description: FAB.
  • Long page with many doubts: PASTOR or 4 Ps.
  • Reader needs educating first: QUEST.
  • You need trust and a human face: Star, Story, Solution.
  • Editing a finished draft: 4 Cs.

  5. 4 Ps, homepage for a home cleaner. Picture: “Imagine walking into a spotless home on Friday.” Promise: “We clean, you do nothing.” Prove: “Rated 4.9 by 300 local families.” Push: “Book this week for 20% off your first clean.”

Scenario workshop: 5 worked scenarios for each framework

Open a framework to see five realistic situations. For each one you get the copy, then how it was solved and why it works. Cover the copy first and try your own version.

AIDA: 5 scenarios

Use when the reader does not know you yet and you must lead them from notice to action.

1. A café wants lunch customers from Instagram.

The copy. Attention: “Another sad desk sandwich?” Interest: “Our soups are made at 7am, five minutes from your office.” Desire: “Hot, filling, ready in two minutes.” Action: “Order for 12:30.”

How and why. Open on a shared annoyance, then add one fact per line. Readers keep going only if line one is about them, and a named time makes acting easy.

2. A webinar for new freelancers (email).

The copy. Attention: “Your first invoice is the hardest.” Interest: “In 45 minutes you’ll learn to price work without guessing.” Desire: “Bring one project and leave with a quote you’re proud of.” Action: “Save your seat.”

How and why. A small time cost and a concrete deliverable make the desire believable.

3. A running-shoe launch email.

The copy. Attention: “Your knees remember every mile.” Interest: “The Cloud 2 has a softer midsole and weighs 240 g.” Desire: “Picture finishing Sunday’s long run still feeling light.” Action: “Try them for 30 days.”

How and why. Pain first, one spec for credibility, a picture of the result, then a trial that removes risk.

4. A dentist landing-page hero.

The copy. Attention: “Scared of the dentist?” Interest: “Quiet tools and unhurried 40-minute visits.” Desire: “Nervous patients are our speciality.” Action: “Book a calm first visit.”

How and why. The opening question qualifies the right reader at once, and the button names the outcome.

5. A beginner guitar course ad.

The copy. Attention: “That guitar has been in the corner for two years.” Interest: “Learn your first song in 7 days, 10 minutes a day.” Desire: “Imagine playing it for friends.” Action: “Start lesson one free.”

How and why. A specific, small plan beats “learn guitar”, and the desire is an imagined moment, not a feature.

PAS: 5 scenarios

Use when the reader already feels the problem and wants it gone.

1. Invoicing software landing page.

The copy. Problem: “Chasing late invoices?” Agitate: “Every reminder costs time and feels awkward.” Solution: “InvoicePal sends polite reminders for you.”

How and why. Name the pain in the owner’s own words, stack the cost they already feel, then give one solution.

2. A standing-desk ad.

The copy. Problem: “Back aching by 3pm?” Agitate: “Hours in a low chair stiffen your neck and drain your focus.” Solution: “Raise your desk in five seconds and work standing.”

How and why. The body detail makes the problem physical, and the solution is quick and concrete.

3. A home-cleaning email.

The copy. Problem: “Weekends disappearing into chores?” Agitate: “Two hours of cleaning is two hours not spent with people.” Solution: “Book a clean from £30 and take Saturday back.”

How and why. Reframes chores as lost time with people, which is the real emotional cost.

4. A cold email from a web freelancer.

The copy. Subject: “Your site takes 6 seconds to load.” Problem: “I tested your homepage on mobile.” Agitate: “Visitors who wait that long often leave before they see your offer.” Solution: “I can get it under 2 seconds. Want a free audit?”

How and why. A measured, personal problem is credible, and the ask is small.

5. An abandoned-cart email for a meal kit.

The copy. Problem: “Still deciding what’s for dinner?” Agitate: “Another night of ‘nothing in the fridge’ is on its way.” Solution: “Your 5-minute dinner box is still in your cart.”

How and why. Meets the customer’s evening moment and points back to the exact item.

BAB: 5 scenarios

Use when the outcome is the selling point, such as fitness, learning or design.

1. A language app email.

The copy. Before: “You know the words but freeze when someone speaks.” After: “You order lunch in Spanish without thinking.” Bridge: “Ten minutes of speaking practice a day.”

How and why. The after scene is specific and ordinary, so it feels reachable.

2. A gym programme ad.

The copy. Before: “You skip workouts because there’s no time.” After: “Thirty minutes, three mornings a week, and you feel stronger.” Bridge: “Our 30-minute plans fit before work.”

How and why. Removes the main objection (time) inside the bridge.

3. A bookkeeping service page.

The copy. Before: “A shoebox of receipts every April.” After: “Taxes done in an afternoon.” Bridge: “Snap receipts all year and we sort them.”

How and why. Contrast between a dreaded ritual and an afternoon makes the value obvious.

4. A skincare product page.

The copy. Before: “Skin that stings after every moisturiser.” After: “Calm, comfortable skin all day.” Bridge: “A fragrance-free barrier cream.”

How and why. Feelings, not ingredients, carry the contrast; the product appears only as the bridge.

5. A café website redesign pitch.

The copy. Before: “Guests can’t find your menu.” After: “They find it and book a table in under a minute.” Bridge: “A clean one-page site, live in two weeks.”

How and why. Gives the owner a clear picture and a timeline.

FAB: 5 scenarios

Use for product pages, bullets and spec sheets: turn every feature into a benefit.

1. A head torch.

The copy. Feature: 300-lumen beam. Advantage: lights the whole path, not just your feet. Benefit: “Run after work and feel safe on dark lanes.”

How and why. Ask “so what?” twice, and stop when you reach a feeling or a situation.

2. An insulated bottle.

The copy. Feature: double-wall vacuum insulation. Advantage: keeps drinks cold for 24 hours. Benefit: “Ice-cold water at the end of a hot hike.”

How and why. The benefit is a moment the buyer can picture.

3. A project-management app.

The copy. Feature: 200+ integrations. Advantage: connects the tools you already use. Benefit: “Stop copying data between apps.”

How and why. Names the daily chore the feature removes.

4. Noise-cancelling headphones.

The copy. Feature: adaptive noise cancelling. Advantage: blocks train rumble and chatter. Benefit: “Read or work on your commute in peace.”

How and why. Ties the feature to the reader’s real situation (the commute).

5. A coffee subscription.

The copy. Feature: roasted to order. Advantage: beans arrive days after roasting. Benefit: “Café-fresh flavour at home.”

How and why. The benefit uses a comparison the customer already understands.

4 Ps: 5 scenarios

Use for homepages and sales letters: Picture, Promise, Prove, Push.

1. A home cleaner.

The copy. Picture: “Imagine walking into a spotless home on Friday.” Promise: “We clean, you do nothing.” Prove: “Rated 4.9 by 300 local families.” Push: “Book this week for 20% off.”

How and why. Start with the desired scene, then make the promise simple, then back it up with a number.

2. An online tutoring service.

The copy. Picture: “Your child closes the laptop saying, ‘That actually made sense.’” Promise: “One-to-one lessons with a qualified teacher.” Prove: “4.8 out of 5 from 640 parents.” Push: “Book the free first lesson.”

How and why. A parent’s emotional win comes first; proof calms doubt; the offer lowers risk.

3. A mobile bike mechanic.

The copy. Picture: “Your bike back, without losing your Saturday.” Promise: “We fix it at your door.” Prove: “Most repairs done within 48 hours.” Push: “Book a repair.”

How and why. Each P answers one doubt: want, how, believe, act.

4. A beginner photography course.

The copy. Picture: “Your photos finally look the way you see them.” Promise: “A 4-week beginner course in manual mode.” Prove: “Every lesson has a practice task and feedback.” Push: “Join the next intake.”

How and why. Proof here is process, which is honest when there are no results yet.

5. A loose-leaf tea shop.

The copy. Picture: “Jasmine tea unfurling on a quiet morning.” Promise: “Loose-leaf tea, delivered fresh.” Prove: “Packed to order in small batches.” Push: “Get 10% off your first tin.”

How and why. Sensory picture first suits an indulgent product.

PASTOR: 5 scenarios

Use for long sales pages: Problem, Amplify, Story and solution, Transformation and testimony, Offer, Response.

1. A fitness coach’s programme.

The copy. P: you’re too tired to train after work. A: skipping becomes a habit, then guilt. S: the coach is a former night-shift nurse who built 20-minute workouts. T: “I train four times a week now.” O: 8-week programme. R: “Join before Sunday.”

How and why. Each step moves the reader one emotional step closer to a decision.

2. A pricing course for freelancers.

The copy. P: you undercharge and resent clients. A: every low quote becomes a habit. S: “I raised my rates three times in a year with one formula.” T: “Maya now books every client at double her old rate.” O: the 4-week Pricing Lab. R: “Enrol before Friday.”

How and why. Story earns the right to sell; the testimony must be real in real life.

3. A budgeting app for couples.

The copy. P: money arguments. A: silence about spending becomes distance. S: a shared view of spending, no spreadsheets. T: “We stopped arguing about the weekly shop.” O: free for 30 days. R: “Start together tonight.”

How and why. Emotional stakes (distance) make a utility app matter.

4. A first-time managers’ course.

The copy. P: you were promoted but nobody taught you to manage. A: small mistakes become team problems. S: the founder nearly quit, then built a weekly 1:1 system. T: “My team now asks me for feedback.” O: 6-week Manager Basics. R: “Join Monday’s cohort.”

How and why. Mirrors the reader’s identity (new manager) so the story feels like theirs.

5. A meal service for shift workers.

The copy. P: eating from a vending machine at 3am. A: poor food makes tired shifts harder. S: chef-made meals that reheat in 3 minutes. T: “I finally eat properly on nights.” O: five meals, £9.50 each. R: “Order by Thursday for Sunday delivery.”

How and why. A specific audience and time make the copy feel written for them.

QUEST: 5 scenarios

Use for webinars, articles and lead magnets: Qualify, Understand, Educate, Stimulate, Transition.

1. A freelancers’ pricing webinar.

The copy. Qualify: “Freelancers who undercharge.” Understand: “Most of us start too low.” Educate: “Pricing by value, not hours, changes this.” Stimulate: “Picture doubling your day rate.” Transition: “Join Thursday’s free session.”

How and why. Qualifying first saves the wrong readers’ time and strengthens the rest.

2. A balcony-gardening checklist.

The copy. Qualify: “Growing tomatoes on a balcony?” Understand: “You’ve seen leaves yellow and don’t know why.” Educate: “Yellow leaves usually mean overwatering or low nitrogen.” Stimulate: “Picture a full harvest by August.” Transition: “Download the one-page checklist.”

How and why. Teaching one real thing builds trust before the ask.

3. A dentist’s blog post ending.

The copy. Qualify: “Putting off a check-up?” Understand: “Nerves are the top reason.” Educate: “Short, quiet visits make it easier.” Stimulate: “Imagine leaving without dread.” Transition: “Book a calm first visit.”

How and why. The article ends with a natural bridge, not a hard sell.

4. A team-software demo invite.

The copy. Qualify: “Managing a team of 5 to 15?” Understand: “Status meetings eat your week.” Educate: “A shared board shows progress without a meeting.” Stimulate: “Get Fridays back.” Transition: “Book a 15-minute demo.”

How and why. Short stages keep a sales message from feeling like one.

5. An email mini-course for bakers.

The copy. Qualify: “Baker with a list but no sales?” Understand: “Newsletters called ‘Newsletter’ get ignored.” Educate: “Lead with the product and a deadline.” Stimulate: “Picture a sold-out Saturday.” Transition: “Start lesson one.”

How and why. Course framing gives value first; the transition invites the next lesson.

Star, Story, Solution: 5 scenarios

Use for About pages, case studies and fundraising: a person, their struggle, the fix.

1. A sleep programme.

The copy. Star: Priya, a nurse. Story: she slept four hours a night for years and tried everything. Solution: the evening routine she built, now our sleep programme.

How and why. A real person makes the abstract benefit human.

2. A founder About page.

The copy. Star: Jon, a baker’s son. Story: ten years watching small bakeries lose customers to delivery apps. Solution: an ordering page any bakery can set up in a day.

How and why. The founder’s story explains why the product exists, which builds trust.

3. A case study for Little Leaf Tea Co.

The copy. Star: Little Leaf, a two-person tea shop. Story: its welcome email was one image with no button. Solution: a short text-led email with an offer and one button (concept project).

How and why. Labelling it a concept project stays honest and still shows your thinking.

4. A freelancer bio.

The copy. Star: Sam, a former barista. Story: every café Sam worked in had great coffee and weak emails. Solution: Sam now writes emails and makes visuals for small food brands.

How and why. A bio that tells a story is remembered; a list of skills is not.

5. A library fundraiser.

The copy. Star: Mr. Hale, 78, volunteers daily. Story: the roof leaks and books sit in buckets. Solution: “£25 buys a roof tile. Give one today.”

How and why. A small, concrete donation unit makes the ask easy to say yes to.

4 Cs (editing checklist): 5 scenarios

Use on any finished draft: is it Clear, Concise, Compelling and Credible?

1. A cold email opening.

The copy. Before: “We leverage innovative synergies to optimise your marketing.” After: “I help small shops write emails customers open.”

How and why. Clear and concise: plain words, one idea, who it helps.

2. An About-page sentence.

The copy. Before: “We are a passionate, results-driven team.” After: “Three designers, one rule: we deliver on the day we promise.”

How and why. Credible: a rule the reader can check replaces empty adjectives.

3. A product description.

The copy. Before: “Premium, high-quality bottle for all your hydration needs.” After: “Keeps drinks cold for 24 hours. Fits any cup holder.”

How and why. Specific facts are both clearer and more believable.

4. An error message.

The copy. Before: “Error 402.” After: “Your card was declined. Try another card or contact your bank.”

How and why. Clear: say what happened and what to do next.

5. An ad headline.

The copy. Before: “The ultimate productivity solution.” After: “Plan your week in 10 minutes.”

How and why. Compelling: a concrete, quick outcome beats a superlative.

How to memorise them and really understand them

  • Learn three first. Spend week one on AIDA, PAS and BAB. Add one new framework every two days after that.
  • Learn the question, not the letters. Each step answers a question in the reader’s head. AIDA: “Is this for me? Is it interesting? Do I want it? What do I do?” PAS: “Do you understand my problem? How bad is it? Can you fix it?” BAB: “Where am I? Where could I be? How do I get there?” If you know the question, you can rebuild the framework from scratch.
  • Use pictures. AIDA: an opera singer (Aida) grabs your eye, charms you, makes you want a ticket, then you buy it. PAS: a plaster (pass the plaster): the cut, rubbing salt in it, then the fix. BAB: a bridge between a grey cliff and a sunny one. 4 Ps: paint, promise, prove, push.
  • Chunk into families. Pain-led: PAS, PASTOR. Journey-led: BAB, Star, Story, Solution. Ladder-led: AIDA, 4 Ps, QUEST. Translators and checkers: FAB, 4 Cs. Nine items become four groups.
  • Reverse-engineer daily. Take three ads or emails and label each line with the step it plays. Five minutes a day is enough.
  • Do the three-way drill. Write the same product in three frameworks (Exercise 4 below). The differences teach you more than any definition.
  • Flashcards with spaced repetition. Front: a situation (“reader doesn’t know they have a problem”). Back: the framework and its steps. Review on days 1, 3, 7 and 14.
  • Teach it out loud. Explain one framework in a 60-second voice note. If you can’t, you haven’t got it yet.
  • Keep a swipe file. Save copy you admire and file it by framework, then build a one-page cheat sheet from it.

A 7-day plan

  • Day 1: AIDA. Day 2: PAS. Day 3: BAB.
  • Day 4: FAB and 4 Ps. Day 5: PASTOR and QUEST.
  • Day 6: Star, Story, Solution and 4 Cs.
  • Day 7: write one product in all of them and pick the winner.

Five rules: be specific, write “you” not “we”, use plain words, cut what doesn’t earn its place, give one CTA.

Exercise 3. Quick-fire: edit ruthlessly

Original: “At our company, we are passionate about providing high-quality, innovative solutions that help our customers to achieve their goals in a timely manner.”

Your task: Cut it to one sentence under 12 words that makes a promise a reader could check.

Show solution and explanation
We deliver your project on time, every time.

24 words became 8. “Passionate”, “innovative” and “solutions” are empty claims anyone can make. “On time” is a promise the reader can test.

Exercise 4. One product, three frameworks

Loop is a language app (fictional). Its hook: 10 minutes a day of speaking practice with an AI conversation partner. Its audience: adults who studied a language at school but freeze when they have to speak.

Your task: Write three short versions of one ad, using AIDA, PAS and BAB. Label each step.

Show solution and explanation
AIDA Attention: You studied Spanish for five years. Interest: So why do you freeze when someone speaks to you? Desire: Loop gives you 10 minutes a day of real speaking practice, with nobody judging. Action: Try your first conversation. PAS Problem: You know the words but can’t say them out loud. Agitate: Every trip and every meeting, you stay quiet and the moment passes. Solution: Ten minutes a day with Loop turns speaking into a habit. BAB Before: You freeze when someone speaks Spanish. After: You order lunch, ask directions and chat without translating in your head. Bridge: Loop’s daily 10-minute speaking practice.

What to notice: the facts are identical, only the order changes. AIDA opens with a surprising question, PAS with the pain, and BAB with the future the reader wants.

How to choose: if your audience already feels the frustration, PAS is fastest. If they need to picture the benefit, use BAB. If you’re unsure, AIDA is the safe default. In practice, run two versions as ads and let the results decide.

Minutes 0 to 3

The anatomy of an email that works

An email is read in a crowded inbox, usually on a phone, in about five seconds. Each part has exactly one job:

PartIts one job
Sender nameEarn trust (a person or brand they recognise)
Subject lineGet the open
Preview textFinish the subject line’s thought, never repeat it
Opening lineKeep them reading
BodyBuild just enough desire or clarity
Call to action (CTA)Get one click or reply

Golden rule: one email, one goal, one CTA. If you list five links, you’ll get zero clicks.

Minutes 3 to 6

Subject lines

Aim for 30 to 50 characters, because phones cut off long ones. Put the important words first.

Five formulas that work

  • Specific benefit: “Cut your invoicing time to 20 minutes”
  • Curiosity with clarity: “The 5-minute habit behind our top seller”
  • Deadline or scarcity (only if true): “Free shipping ends tonight”
  • Personal and plain: “Quick question about your Shopify store”
  • Question: “Still exporting spreadsheets by hand?”

Avoid

  • Vague labels (“Newsletter”, “Update”)
  • Clickbait the email doesn’t pay off. It destroys trust.
  • ALL CAPS, repeated “!!!”, and spammy words like “FREE!!! Act now”

Preview text tip: Subject “Your cart is waiting” + preview “Still thinking about the linen shirt? It’s selling fast in your size.” Together they tell one story.

Minutes 6 to 9

Body copy and the CTA

  1. Open with the reader, not you. Skip “We’re excited to announce…”. Start with their situation or the point.
  2. Write short. One idea per paragraph, 1 to 3 lines each. Many readers scan.
  3. Write to one person. Use “you” and read it aloud. If you’d never say it, rewrite it.
  4. Use a button-style CTA that says what happens: “Get my free sample”, not “Submit” or “Click here”.
  5. Add a P.S. It’s one of the most-read parts. Restate the offer or deadline.

Quick test: Cover everything except the subject line and the CTA button. Do they still make sense together? If yes, the email is focused.

Module 3: Landing pages and their types

The page that turns visitors into customers: eight types, each with its format and copy jobs, plus two realistic rewrites.

  • 0–6 Lesson

Minutes 0 to 6

Landing and sales pages

A landing page has one job: persuade one visitor to take one action. Visitors decide in seconds, so the top of the page (the hero) carries most of the weight.

The hero formula

  • Headline: the outcome the visitor wants, in plain words
  • Subheadline: who it’s for, how it works, or the key proof
  • Button: says what happens next (“Book a repair”)
  • Proof nearby: rating, number of customers, a logo or a quote

A reliable page order

  1. Hero
  2. The problem, in the visitor’s words
  3. Your solution, shown in 3 steps
  4. Benefits (not a feature dump)
  5. Proof: reviews, numbers, results
  6. Objections answered (FAQ, guarantee)
  7. Final CTA, the same one as the hero

Objections decide the sale

Most visitors leave because of a doubt, not a lack of interest. The usual four: price, trust, effort and fit. Find them in exit surveys, support emails and reviews, then answer them on the page.

5-second test: show the hero to a stranger for five seconds. If they can’t say what you offer and who it’s for, rewrite it.

Reference

Module 4: Ads, brand voice and audio

Written ads, a consistent voice, and the audio and voice-ad lab.

Minutes 0 to 6

Ad copy and brand voice

An ad interrupts someone who didn’t ask for it. It has about two seconds to earn attention, so it has three parts: a hook, one idea of body copy, and a clear CTA.

Four angles to test

  • Pain: name the frustration
  • Outcome: show life after the problem is solved
  • Proof: a customer quote or number
  • Curiosity: a surprising fact, with a payoff

Practical limits

  • Meta ads: the first line or two of the main text is all many people see, so lead with the hook.
  • Google search ads: short headlines (about 30 characters each) and descriptions (about 90). Check the current limits in the ad platform.
  • Test one thing at a time: a new angle, not a new angle and a new picture.

Brand voice in 3 minutes

A voice guide is three adjectives plus a clear do and don’t for each. Example: Friendly, not silly. Clear, not blunt. Confident, not pushy. Then the rule of voice: the same brand should sound the same in an ad, an error message and an email.

Rule: an ad promises, the landing page delivers. If the ad says “free”, the page must say “free” in the same words.

Part 2

The audio and audio-ad copywriting lab

Objective: write copy that converts when it is spoken aloud, heard on the go or triggered through a voice assistant. The key tool is the Ear Test: read it out loud and check rhythm, pacing and feeling.

Part A: master breakdown (how a pro thinks)

Product: a habit-tracking productivity app.
Download TaskMaster today to organize your tasks, increase efficiency, and reach your daily goals.
“Be honest: how many times have you written a to-do list today… only to lose it under a pile of coffee mugs? [Short pause] Stop letting mental clutter steal your focus. TaskMaster puts every project, deadline and idea into one clean space. Download it free in your app store.”

The hook (relatable pain): the pro opens with a human struggle (coffee mugs, mental clutter) instead of features. On audio, sell the feeling first.

Pacing and acoustics: notice [Short pause]. Audio copywriters write in breaths, with sentences short enough that the listener never feels rushed.

CTA simplicity: listeners can’t click while driving or running, so skip long URLs and codes. Say “Download it free in your app store.”

Part B: three levels of challenge

Level 1: Beginner. The podcast host-read

Skill focus: Replace stiff advertising jargon with conversational, spoken cadence.

Scenario: A snack brand wants to sponsor a weekly fitness podcast.

Try our organic protein bars. Non-GMO, 20 grams of plant-based protein, and delicious chocolate flavor. Visit snackbrand.com for 10% off.

Your mission: Rewrite this as a 15-second host-read that sounds like a real podcast host talking to listeners.

Stop and try it: say your draft out loud before you open the solution.

Show expert solution and breakdown
“Post-workout crash? I’ve been keeping these organic plant-protein bars in my gym bag all month. They actually taste like real chocolate, not cardboard. Grab yours at snackbrand.com with code PODCAST.”

How: It names a specific moment (“post-workout crash”), adds a self-deprecating comparison (“not cardboard”) for authenticity, and uses a simple, memorable code.

Why: Listeners tune out ads that sound like press releases. A personal recommendation from a trusted host usually earns more attention and action.

Level 2: Intermediate. The smart-speaker voice ad

Skill focus: Write for zero-screen settings and conversational search intent.

Scenario: A local pet grooming business wants customers who ask voice assistants (Siri, Alexa) in their cars or kitchens.

Best Pet Grooming in Seattle. Open Mon-Sat 9am-6pm. Call 555-0199 for bookings.

Your mission: Write a conversational voice response for when someone asks: “Who is a good pet groomer near me that has Saturday openings?”

Stop and try it: say your draft out loud before you open the solution.

Show expert solution and breakdown
“Paws and Claws Grooming is five minutes away and has an open slot this Saturday at 2 PM. They specialise in gentle coat trims. Would you like me to book that appointment for you right now?”

How: It answers the implied question (distance and Saturday availability) and ends with a one-step voice action: “Would you like me to book that?”

Why: Voice search is transactional. The reply can’t just list hours. It must give a solution and make the next step effortless.

Level 3: Advanced. The multi-sensory interactive audio flow

Skill focus: Orchestrate a multi-step audio experience where the listener responds with sound or voice.

Scenario: A travel-booking app runs an in-app audio campaign for people listening to travel podcasts while packing.

Compare flights and hotels with the Wanderlust App. Download now.

Your mission: Write a 30-second script with a sound-effect cue, an emotional hook and a voice-activated prompt. Use stage directions such as [SFX], [Host tone] and [Pause].

Stop and try it: say your draft out loud before you open the solution.

Show expert solution and breakdown
(0-5 sec) [SFX: airport murmur and a boarding-pass scanner beep] (Host, excited tone): “Hearing that sound yet? If your suitcase is still sitting on your bed half-empty, take a deep breath.” (5-20 sec): “Flights shouldn’t cost a fortune, and planning shouldn’t give you a headache. With the Wanderlust App you can compare flights and hotels in 10 seconds flat.” (20-30 sec): “If you’re listening on a smart speaker, just say: ‘Alexa, ask Wanderlust to find weekend flights to Miami.’ Or tap the screen if you’re holding your phone. Let’s get you on a plane.”

How: Sound design in the first seconds creates a mental image (airport) before any word. The CTA is dual: a voice command for smart speakers and a tap for phone users.

Why: Advanced audio copy treats sound effects and silence as punctuation. Bridging voice activation with a normal mobile action reaches both hands-busy and eyes-free listeners.

Self-grading rubric

  1. The out-loud test. Did I read it start to finish without tripping? Pass if yes.
  2. The anti-jargon rule. Did I replace corporate buzzwords with human, conversational pain points? Pass if yes.
  3. The zero-friction CTA. Can a listener remember or do the next step without a complex URL? Pass if yes.

Module 5: Your portfolio case study

Package your best work, explain your thinking, and write an offer clients can answer.

  • 0–6 Lesson
  • 6–15 Two exercises
Minutes 0 to 6

Turn your practice into a portfolio

Clients rarely hire the person with the most pieces. They hire the person who explains clearly what they did and why. A case study shows your thinking.

Case study structure

  1. Title and one-line summary: what the project was
  2. The challenge: the brief and the problem
  3. Your approach: two or three choices and the reason for each
  4. The work: before and after, or the final pieces
  5. Result or intended result: real numbers if you have them, otherwise what it’s designed to do
  6. Tools and what you’d do next

Be honest

Label concept and spec projects clearly. Mention AI use plainly. Never invent clients, numbers or testimonials. Honest concept work looks better than a doubtful claim.

Package it

  • Choose 5 or 6 of your best pieces, not everything.
  • Write one offer sentence: who you help, with what, and the result.
  • Put a contact button on every page.
  • Send the portfolio link in a short, specific pitch (see Module 2, Exercise 4).

Final project: complete one mini campaign for one brand: a landing-page hero, a 3-email welcome sequence, three ads, and a photo set. Publish it as a single case study.
